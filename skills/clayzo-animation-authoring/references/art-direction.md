# Art direction

Read this before choosing a single shape. The recipes in the other references
say how to build; this says what to build, and it is where pieces that were
technically correct still looked like nobody chose anything.

## Why the reference pieces work

Four references, four studios, one behaviour. Each picks **one graphic idea**
and commits to it at **full scale** in **flat, saturated colour**, with the
mark or the type as the only object and **no explanation**:

- A five-colour mark, tiled edge to edge at 45°, floods from grey to colour
  from a single point and drains back. No words. The pattern *is* the frame.
- Purple pixel chains, an acid-yellow 24-point burst, orange dots and one word
  on black. Every element is oversized; the burst is a third of the frame.
- One wordmark cut into eight bars, on a black field over a blue one, cascading
  in. Nothing else exists.
- Two social cards whose hero band is a grid of giant rounded cells in one
  blue and one orange. The UI chrome is placeholder bars, not copy.

None of them has a tagline, a caption, a footer, a stat row, a drop shadow, a
gradient, a dot field at 7 % contrast, a pen drawing a line, or a small
object in the middle of an empty ground. That list is what makes an
animation look generated. Do not do any of it unless the brief asks.

## The choice

A brief of one sentence with no colours, no copy and no reference is not a
reason to be safe; it is the invitation to make the choice for them. Make it
in this order, in writing, before authoring:

1. **One graphic verb.** Flood, tile, cascade, zoom, snap, orbit, split,
   stack, stripe. The whole piece is that verb applied to one system. If you
   need two verbs, you have two pieces.
2. **One geometry language.** Circles and pills, or squares, or bars, or one
   letterform. The backdrop, the hero and the punctuation are all built from
   it, so the frame reads as one object.
3. **A committed palette**, 3–5 flat colours, at least two saturated, on a
   field that is itself a colour or pure black/white/cream. No gradients, no
   shadows, no muted "tasteful" mids as the main colours. When the brand's
   colours are given, use them at full strength and at full scale; when they
   are not, pick a world and own it:
   - *Acid on black*: `#101010` · `#F0FE48` · `#691CB9` · `#FF8827` · `#1BD660`
   - *Primaries on white*: `#FDFDFD` · `#F24E1E` · `#FF7262` · `#A259FF` · `#1ABCFE` · `#0ACF83`
   - *Bauhaus paper*: `#F2E8D5` · `#D63A2F` · `#1F4FBF` · `#F2B705` · `#1B1B1B`
   - *Swiss red*: `#E63122` · `#FFFFFF` · `#111111`
   - *Riso*: `#FF48B0` · `#0078BF` · `#FFE800` · `#F4F1EA`
   - *Field and ink*: a single saturated field (`#3A3AFF`, `#FF8A2B`, `#0ACF83`)
     with white and one dark.
   A dark ground with one accent is allowed only when the brief says dark
   mode, and then the accent goes big — not a 3 px caret.
4. **Scale before anything moves.** The system fills the frame: high-contrast
   content spans at least 80 % of the width; the hero alone is at least a
   third of the frame's shorter edge. Small is the default failure.
5. **Words: one or none.** A wordmark or one word at ≥ 12 % of the frame
   height, or nothing. No taglines, captions, footers, dates, stats or
   placeholder bars unless the brief asks for copy — and if it asks for
   numbers, the numbers are the hero and there are at most three.
6. **Punch.** One moment in the first 0.7 s with physical energy — a
   pop-plateau-snap, a flood with a front, a zoom from the axis — and an
   entrance order that reads as designed. Fade-and-settle is for type, not
   for the hero.

## The poster test

Render the settled frame at 360 px. If it would not work as a poster on a
wall — one object, one palette, legible from across a room — the piece is
not ready, whatever the motion does. Then render the first 0.7 s as a sheet:
if you cannot say what the graphic verb is from those frames alone, neither
can the viewer.

## Illustration is not animation

A receipt printing, a to-do list writing itself, a cup of coffee, a pen: these
are illustrations of the brief. They can be charming and they are never the
reference standard, because the eye reads the picture and stops. A graphic
system — the brand's shapes, tiled, coloured, moving as one — keeps the eye
moving and belongs to the brand. Prefer the system; keep the illustration
only if it *is* the system (the mark tiled, the letterform striped).

## Anti-patterns, named

- Dark ground, one coral or blue accent, thin type, a small card. The
  generated look.
- A backdrop nobody can see. If it is there, it is there.
- Explaining: captions under the mark, "launching this fall", a footer line.
- Two ideas (a pen *and* a ripple *and* a drop): keep the strongest.
- Muted palettes chosen to be inoffensive. The references are loud.
- Gradients, glows, drop shadows and vignettes as a substitute for colour.
- A logo reveal that draws the logo with a pen. Flood it, snap it, tile it,
  assemble it from its own parts.
